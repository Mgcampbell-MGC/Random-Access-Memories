## 1. THREE CORRECTIONS, AND THE SECOND ONE IS SEVEN MONTHS OLD

### ★ CORRECTION 1 — I told the agents Canada and Australia effectively ban cold B2B email. Wrong.

**CASL s.6(1) does require consent — and s.10(9)(b) creates IMPLIED consent where:**

> *"the person to whom the message is sent has **conspicuously published**… the electronic address… the
> publication is not accompanied by a statement that the person does not wish to receive unsolicited
> commercial electronic messages… and **the message is relevant to the person's business, role, functions or
> duties in a business or official capacity**"*

**Australia's Spam Act 2003 Sch. 2 subcl. 4(2) has the same conspicuous-publication exception** *(exact wording
**UNVERIFIED** — legislation.gov.au and AustLII both refused the agent; structure sourced to ACMA's own
guidance)*.

> ### THE CONSPICUOUS-PUBLICATION LAW — **all five target jurisdictions permit unsolicited commercial email to a business address the recipient published themselves, provided the message is relevant to their role and carries an opt-out. The regimes differ in WHO CARRIES THE BURDEN OF PROOF, not in whether the door is open.**

*(**Germany is the known exception and was not checked.** UWG §7 is materially stricter. Treat DACH as closed.)*

### ★★ CORRECTION 2 — THE EU GRANTED BRAZIL DATA ADEQUACY ON 26 JANUARY 2026

**Implementing Decision (EU) 2026/179.** Brazil now sits on the Commission's adequacy list alongside Japan,
Korea and the UK. EDPB Opinion 28/2025 preceded it.

> **A European firm sending client files to a processor in São Paulo now needs NO SCCs, NO transfer impact
> assessment, NO Article 46 safeguard. The objection *"we can't send this outside the EEA"* has been gone for
> seven months and this file never registered it.**

**This overturns a structural kill recorded EARLIER TODAY** — the S3 agent closed continuous EU sanctions
screening on precisely the SCC-plus-TIA onboarding conversation that no longer exists. **Every EU-facing
white-label candidate previously killed or discounted on data-transfer grounds must be re-examined.**

**The UK did NOT follow.** No UK adequacy regulation or data bridge for Brazil was found; UK→Brazil transfers
still need an IDTA plus a transfer risk assessment. **Plan for EU ≠ UK.** *(Negative from absence of
evidence — confirm before relying.)*

### ★ CORRECTION 3 — Pix is NOT free for her, and I wrote that it was

**BCB Resolution 19/2020, from the Central Bank's own English text:**

| **Art. 3** | Charging a natural person is **prohibited** for remittance for transfer/purchase, and for **receipt for the purpose of transfer** |
| **Art. 4** | The institution **MAY** charge a natural person *"as a result of **receiving funds for the purpose of purchasing**"*, and **may charge a legal entity for remittance AND receipt** |

> **Receiving Pix as payment for a sale is the one case the regulation expressly permits the bank to charge
> for — for every legal form she could take.** *(Observed: PagBank advertises "PIX 0% nos primeiros 30 dias" —
> free as a promotion, priced after.)*

**What survives is still real and still valuable: no acquirer risk review, no chargeback dispute to argue by
telephone, settlement in seconds.** That is genuinely better than card acquiring for a written-only operator.
**But the per-transaction cost is PSP-specific and UNVERIFIED.** `THE_MARKETPLACE_CENSUS.md` is corrected.

## 3. ★ THE FIELD INSPECTION — the CNPJ email fill rate, measured rather than assumed

**The file's own rule is that a field existing is not a field populated.** *(The UK FSA register has a phone
column with 0 of 200 filled.)* **So the agent measured it** — generating random CNPJ base numbers, computing
both check digits, and querying each live. **A random sample of the register, not a convenience sample.**

| | |
|---|---|
| **N sampled** | **32** |
| **Email populated, all records** | **20/32 = 62%** |
| **Telephone populated** | **27/32 = 84%** |
| `SITUAÇÃO CADASTRAL = ATIVA` | 9/32 |
| **Email populated, active only** | **8/9 — ANECDOTAL, N=9, do not quote** |
| Domains seen | gmail ×13, hotmail ×2, yahoo.com.br ×2, own company domain ×3 |

**And the trap, which is the most useful part:** **mirrors silently disagree.** For the same CNPJ,
**minhareceita.org and brasilapi.com.br both return `email: null`, while publica.cnpj.ws and receitaws.com.br
return the real address.** **The agent's first run of 80 records returned 0/80 emails and was pure artefact.**

> **N=32 through a mirror is not enough to build on — the Receita bulk host serves a JavaScript client and
> could not be reached. A human with a browser must download the real `Estabelecimentos` file and measure the
> fill rate filtered to ATIVA and to the target CNAE.**

> ### THE CONTACTABLE-REGISTER LAW — of NINE national registers now field-inspected across six countries, **TWO publish email**: the Australian celebrant register (~58% of 50) and the Brazilian CNPJ dataset (~62% of 32). **The Australian one has 10.832 records and an A$100–400/year wallet. The Brazilian one has the entire national economy.** The best list this project has ever found is in her own country, in her own language, free, and legal to email.

**Most addresses are gmail or hotmail — the owner's personal inbox.** Good for reach, **and it means these are
unambiguously personal data of an identified natural person, so LGPD applies with full force** and the
"generic address of a legal person" carve-out does not rescue her.

**Brazil's legal position, stated plainly:** **there is no Brazilian anti-spam statute** — PL 2186/2003 was
never enacted, and CAPEM is a 2009 self-regulation code, not law. What governs is **LGPD art. 7º IX**
(legítimo interesse, available), **art. 10** (strictly necessary data, transparency), **art. 9º III–IV**
(specific purpose, controller identity and contact — *the Brazilian analogue of CAN-SPAM's postal address;
it requires an identifiable controller, not a face*), and **art. 18** (rights).

**The real exposure, named rather than buried: art. 7º §3º.** *"O tratamento de dados pessoais cujo acesso é
público deve considerar a finalidade, a boa-fé e o interesse público que justificaram sua disponibilização."*
**The CNPJ email was published for TAX ADMINISTRATION, not marketing.** §7º permits repurposing *"observados
os propósitos legítimos e específicos"* — **so it is not forbidden, but it is not automatic, and a naive bulk
blast is exactly the fact pattern §3º was written against.** *(No ANPD enforcement action against unsolicited
B2B email was found — **absence of evidence, not evidence of absence.** Ceiling: 2% of Brazilian revenue,
capped at R$50M.)*

## 4. TWO NEW LAWS ABOUT THE CHANNELS THEMSELVES

### ★ THE PROVIDER-AUP LAW

**Gmail and Outlook both publish hard requirements** — SPF+DKIM+DMARC above 5.000/day, spam rate below
**0,30%**, one-click unsubscribe, with Microsoft **hard-rejecting** since 5 May 2025:
`550 5.7.515 Access denied, sending domain does not meet the required authentication level`.

**But the actual killer is elsewhere.** The standard cold-email stack is N throwaway domains on Google
Workspace seats — and **Google Workspace's Acceptable Use Policy prohibits using the service *"to generate,
distribute, publish or facilitate unsolicited mass email, promotions, advertisements, or other solicitations
('spam')."***

> ### THE PROVIDER-AUP LAW — **cold email is legal under the statutes of every market in this file and simultaneously prohibited by the terms of the mailbox provider used to send it. The regulator is not the risk; the VENDOR is.** She is not one complaint away from a fine — she is one complaint away from losing the sending infrastructure, and re-warming costs weeks she has already spent.

*(Tooling: Instantly Growth **$47/mo**, Hypergrowth **$97/mo**, lead data a separate meter from $47.
**A US/UK operation lands at $150–300/month. The same operation into Brazil off the free CNPJ dataset needs no
lead-data spend at all and lands under $100/month.**)*

### ★ THE DISCLOSED-DEPARTMENT LAW — the invisible department, half-killed and half-rescued

**AICPA Code of Professional Conduct ET §1.150.040 .02, verbatim:**

> *"**before disclosing confidential client information to a third-party service provider, the member should
> inform the client, preferably in writing**, that the member may use a third-party service provider. If the
> client objects… the member either should not use the third-party service provider… or should decline to
> perform the engagement."*

**§1.700.040 .02 then gives the firm a real escape:** it must **either** enter a confidentiality contract with
the provider **or** obtain specific client consent.

> ### THE DISCLOSED-DEPARTMENT LAW — **the invisible department survives, but only half of it. Her IDENTITY can stay invisible: a confidentiality contract removes the need for client consent. Her EXISTENCE cannot.** Any pitch promising the buyer that *nobody will ever know* is **asking a CPA to breach their own code — and the sophisticated buyers will know it.**

**And §1.150.040 .03 carves out the escape worth mining:** no client notification is required where the
provider supplies *"**administrative support services**… (for example, record storage, software application
hosting, or authorized e-file tax transmittal services)."*

> **Positioning the work as ADMINISTRATIVE SUPPORT rather than professional-service assistance moves it
> outside the disclosure rule entirely. That is a live product-shaping constraint, not a legal quibble.**

*(**US individual tax prep is separately closed:** **26 CFR §301.7216-3** — a US preparer *"may not obtain
consent to disclose the taxpayer's social security number"* to a preparer outside the US for 1040-series
clients, and must redact it before any offshore disclosure.)*

## 5. ★ THE SOURCING LAW — a distribution constraint disguised as a tax one

**Two IRS facts, combined:**

1. **There is no US–Brazil income tax treaty.** Brazil does not appear in the IRS's own treaty A-to-Z list.
2. **Personal-service income is sourced where the services are PERFORMED** — *"regardless of where the contract
   was made, or the place of payment, or the residence of the payer."* **Royalties are sourced where the
   property is USED.**

**Amazon KDP confirms it from the payer's side:** US-sale royalties are subject to **30% US withholding**,
reducible only *"if your country of permanent residence has an income tax treaty with the United States."*

> ### THE SOURCING LAW — **work performed in Brazil is foreign-source and reaches her whole. A royalty from a US payer is US-source and reaches her at 70 cents on the dollar, with no treaty relief available to a Brazilian. Every "build it once and collect royalties" channel is taxed 30% before she sees it; every "she does the work in São Paulo" channel is not.**
>
> ### **SELL SERVICES. NEVER LICENSE IP INTO THE UNITED STATES.**

**This independently re-kills the royalty-marketplace family from a direction `ROYALTY_MARKETPLACES_killed.md`
never considered — and it is a hard reason to prefer Brazilian-domestic revenue over US royalty revenue even
at a lower headline price.** *(It also compounds with `THE_TAX_ANSWER.md`'s finding that royalties outside the
company's registered object are taxed on a 100% base in Brazil: **a US royalty stream can be hit at 30%
American withholding AND ~34% Brazilian tax.**)*

### What would have to be true — and #2 is the hard one

1. **The 62% fill rate must hold at source**, on the real bulk file, filtered to ATIVA and to the target CNAE.
   **If it collapses below ~30% the channel degrades to phone-only and dies on C1.**
2. **She must clear R$1.000+/month per customer.** Observed Brazilian self-serve B2B SaaS: **Bling R$60 /
   R$120 / R$650**, ContaAzul ~R$90–250 *(indicative)*. At R$150 she needs **385 customers** — not serviceable
   at 30 h/week. At R$1.500, **39**. **So the buyer's comparison must be A SALARY, NOT A SUBSCRIPTION.**
   > **Under THE CONSTRAINT-SIDE LAW that points at a buyer whose volume is set by the BRAZILIAN FISCAL
   > CALENDAR — obrigações acessórias, SPED, eSocial, DCTFWeb, EFD-Reinf — and whose cost is their own hours.
   > *Escritórios de contabilidade* are the canonical instance, they are enumerable by CNAE in that very
   > dataset, and they carry email.**
3. **LGPD art. 7º §3º must be handled deliberately** — a documented legitimate-interest assessment following
   ANPD's own three-phase test, controller identity in every message, one-click opt-out, suppression list.
   **An afternoon of work, and skipping it is the difference between a lawful channel and an ANPD file.**
4. **Reply handling must stay written all the way to payment.** **Brazilian SMEs default to WhatsApp and to
   phoning back. The funnel needs a written-only path designed in from message one, or C1 breaks at the exact
   moment the channel starts working.**

### The test

> **Pull 200 active establishments in ONE CNAE. Send each one message. Count how many reply in writing, and
> how many name a NUMBER when asked what the task currently costs them in hours.** R$0 for the list, under
> R$300 for infrastructure. **Twenty replies out of 200 is the bar.**
>
> **Run it against TWO different CNAEs** — because the finding that matters is not *"does cold email work"*
> but **WHICH constraint-side Brazilian buyer answers a stranger in writing.**
