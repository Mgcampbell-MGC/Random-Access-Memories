# Brief: find a digital product for a specific founder

You are being asked to find a **business idea**, not to evaluate one. Your output is a
recommendation you would stake your reputation on, with the reasoning that makes it checkable.

Work from evidence. Where you assert a number, a price, a market size, a legal rule or a
competitor's existence, either cite a source you actually consulted or mark it **UNVERIFIED**.
Do not invent statistics. An honest "I could not confirm this" is worth more than a confident
figure that dissolves on contact.

---

## 1. The founder — treat these as fixed facts, not preferences

- Woman, ~26, based in São Paulo, Brazil. Native Portuguese. Written English is functional.
  **She will not do unscripted live calls in English.** She is comfortable on the phone in
  Portuguese.
- Not a trained developer, but genuinely ships working software by directing an AI coding agent.
  Treat "can produce working software" as **TRUE**. Treat "can personally write and debug code
  unaided" as **FALSE**.
- Prior career: modelling, and luxury/premium brand events. No professional credential in law,
  medicine, accountancy, engineering or finance.
- Has a new Brazilian company (CNPJ) and access to a **US company**, so USD/GBP/EUR card payments,
  Stripe, Paddle, Lemon Squeezy and similar are all available. Payment and payout are solved.
- Capital: a few hundred US dollars of test budget. **No employees, no contractors, no inventory,
  no audience, no email list, no following, no existing customers.**
- **Faceless is permanent.** No camera, no personal brand, no influencer or creator work, no
  webinars, no podcast, no building an audience as the route to market. This is not shyness to be
  designed around; it is a fixed constraint.
- **Her personal São Paulo network is off limits as a sales channel.** It is a non-renewable asset
  and is excluded on purpose. Do not propose anything that depends on who she knows.

## 2. The target

**R$30.000 per month net to the founder**, roughly **US$7.400–8.300/month in revenue**. It must be
reachable within a year and it must be sustainable — a business that could theoretically produce
that once, then exhaust its market, does not qualify.

## 3. What the product must be

A **digital product, sold self-serve, worldwide.** The buyer discovers it, buys it and receives it
without Sol performing any per-customer step in English. The *content* may be specific to one
country, industry, profession or language — narrow content is fine, even good. The *transaction*
must be global and automatic.

Support load must not grow into a job. Assume support scales with the number of units sold, not
with revenue: a $29 product sold 300 times a month generates ten times the support burden of a $297
product sold 30 times for the same money.

## 4. Economics already established — use these, don't re-derive them

- **A click costs the same whatever the product costs.** So customer acquisition cost does not fall
  when you lower the price; only the margin available to pay it does. At roughly US$1,50 per click
  with acquisition capped at 30% of price, a $29 product needs ~17% conversion (not achievable), $97
  needs ~5,2%, $197 needs ~2,5%, $297 needs ~1,7%. Cold, high-intent landing pages convert at about
  1–5%. **Paid traffic is generally only viable from about US$97 upward.**
- Some verticals price clicks at US$10–30, which removes paid acquisition as an option there
  entirely regardless of product price.
- Cold outbound email legality varies and matters: the US permits opt-out B2B commercial email under
  CAN-SPAM if its formal requirements are met; the UK is workable for corporate subscribers under
  PECR; Canada's CASL is consent-based and much stricter; Germany is effectively closed under UWG
  §7(2), which competitors themselves can enforce.

## 5. Post-mortems — three real attempts that died

These are **evidence about failure modes**. They are **not a map of where to search.** Do not
propose variants of them, do not stay in their industries, and do not treat their shape as the
shape of a good answer. Read them for the mechanism of death, then think independently.

**A. A monitoring product for US charities with a public compliance defect.**
Fully designed, documented and verified before anyone measured whether the buyer had money. A
sample of 150 delinquent organisations joined to their filed revenue found roughly 70% under
US$50.000 of annual revenue. Died on the buyer's wallet.
*Mechanism: a publicly visible compliance defect selects for organisations that are defective
because they are under-resourced. The signal that identifies the prospect also identifies their
inability to pay.*

**B. A reference product that detected an error in a professional database.**
The defect was real and measured correctly: a large share of the technical codes cited in an
official ruling archive no longer existed in the current schedule. It died anyway, because (i) the
government's own published guidance already instructed users to perform exactly that check, free,
in seconds; (ii) the filing system downstream automatically rejected the bad value, so the error
cost minutes of rework rather than money; (iii) the headline statistic, while arithmetically true,
counted administrative churn with no legal consequence; and (iv) the total addressable market —
about 20.000 possible buyers, purchasing once — could never reach the income target.
*Mechanism: an unserved problem is often unserved because it costs nobody anything. "Nobody sells
this" is evidence of low value at least as often as it is evidence of opportunity.*

**C. A self-help product for small employers facing an employment tribunal claim.**
Died on four things: the buyer could not be identified at the moment of need, because filed claims
are confidential and only judgments are published — after the case is over; a regulated professional
performed the same specific task for roughly the same price, with insurance behind it; the corpus it
was built on turned out to carry no precedential weight; and the governing statute was being
rewritten on dated commencement schedules while the product would be on sale.
*Mechanism: a buyer who faces the problem once, cannot be found in advance, and can hire a regulated
human for the same money is not a market.*

## 6. The questions that have actually killed things

Any candidate you propose must survive these. Answer them **for your own idea, in your own output**,
with evidence.

1. **Who loses a specific amount of money when this problem goes unsolved, and by what mechanism?**
   Name the person and the number. If the honest answer is "some wasted time" or "they'd feel
   better", stop.
2. **Is something already catching this for free — automatically?** Government systems, platform
   validation, an included service, a professional the buyer already pays. Check before you fall in
   love.
3. **Can the buyer be reached?** Is there a public, timely, lawful signal that a specific buyer needs
   this *now*? Or does the need arise invisibly, so the only route is winning search traffic against
   incumbents with budgets? An invisible trigger is close to fatal for a small operator.
4. **Does the same buyer need it again?** Once-a-decade purchases mean paying full acquisition cost
   for every sale forever, with no referrals and no retention.
5. **What does the human substitute cost?** Find the actual published price of a professional doing
   this specific task. If a regulated, insured human charges the same or less, you have no product.
6. **Can the buyer tell whether the output is right?** After they open it, is there something they
   can check — two values that differ, a date, a name, a number they recognise? If the product's only
   claim is "trust me, you're non-compliant", it is dead on arrival.
7. **Does the arithmetic reach the target?** Show it: price × units needed per month, against a
   defensible estimate of how many buyers exist and how many can realistically be reached per year.
8. **What is the shelf life?** If the underlying rules change on a published schedule, a static
   product misstates them and the customer finds out.
9. **Is everything you'd bundle lawfully redistributable commercially?** Do not assume public
   equals free to resell. As one concrete example: in the UK, first-instance tribunal decisions sit
   under an open licence permitting commercial reuse, while binding appellate judgments sit under a
   different licence that expressly forbids computational analysis without a separate application.
   Licensing traps are common and specific — check the actual terms of whatever you'd ship.
10. **Does it need a credential Sol cannot hold?** Selling information and tools is broadly lawful;
    performing a regulated activity is not. Know which side your idea is on, in the specific
    jurisdiction you target.

## 7. Rules

**Hard — do not break these.**
- No live English calls, ever, in the sale, delivery or support path.
- No camera, no personal brand, no audience-building as the route to market.
- No selling through her personal network.
- No activity requiring a professional licence or credential she does not hold, in the target market.
- No bundling of third-party content that is not lawfully redistributable for commercial use.
- No asserting that a buyer is legally obliged to do or pay something without real authority for it.
- No outbound channel that is unlawful in the market it targets.
- No employees, no inventory, no capital requirement beyond a few hundred dollars to start.

**Soft — break any of these if you can justify it.** These are patterns drawn from failures, not
laws of nature:
- Sell to a professional who repeats the task, rather than an individual who meets it once.
- Price at US$97 or above.
- Prefer businesses to consumers.
- Avoid markets where the buyer's problem is visible to you only after it is too late to help.
- Avoid one-time purchases with no natural repeat or renewal.
- Prefer buyers who compare your price to an hour of a professional's time rather than to a book.

**If you break a soft rule, say so explicitly:** name the rule, explain what makes this case
different, and state what evidence would prove you wrong. A well-argued rule-break is more valuable
than timid compliance. Several of these rules exist only because one idea died a particular way,
and any of them could be wrong in general.

## 8. How to think — and how not to

The founder's own critique of AI idea-generation, which you should take seriously:

> "AI often kills business ideas because it is much better at listing risks than discovering hidden
> opportunities. It defaults to consultant mode. **It confuses 'not proven' with 'bad.'** Almost
> every new business looks uncertain before testing."

So apply this distinction:

- **Structural objections kill.** An identical product already shipping at a lower price; a law
  making it illegal; a list of buyers that does not exist and cannot be built; a free automatic
  system that already solves it; arithmetic that cannot reach the target.
- **Adoption objections do not kill.** Will they pay this much, will the page convert, do they know
  they have the problem, will they trust an unknown seller. These are unknowns, not defects. If you
  raise one, you must attach a way to find out that costs under US$200 and takes under two weeks,
  with a threshold that would settle it.

Do not produce a risk list. Do not hedge every sentence. Commit to an answer.

**Do not anchor on the shapes above.** The dead attempts were all reference or monitoring products
built on public data. That is one possible shape among many — it is not the brief. Software,
templates, datasets, tools, guides, generators, directories, calculators, feeds and things with no
existing category are all in scope, provided they are digital, self-serve and globally sellable.

Deliberately generate candidates from **structurally different shapes**, then choose. If your final
three all resemble each other, you have not searched, you have elaborated.

## 9. Do not propose

Variants of the three dead attempts: US charity registration compliance; tariff or customs
classification reference material; employment tribunal defence for employers. They are closed.

## 10. Output

**Part 1 — the field.** Six to ten candidates, one short paragraph each: what it is, who pays, what
it costs, why it might work. Show your range. Include at least two that are structurally unlike the
others.

**Part 2 — the shortlist.** The three strongest, each answered against all ten questions in §6, with
sources and explicit UNVERIFIED tags. Include the arithmetic to the income target as a visible
calculation.

**Part 3 — the recommendation.** One idea. Say plainly why it beats the other two. State the single
assumption most likely to be wrong, and the cheapest check that would settle it — what it costs,
how long it takes, and what result would make you abandon the idea.

**Part 4 — what you could not verify.** Everything you asserted without a source, listed honestly.
